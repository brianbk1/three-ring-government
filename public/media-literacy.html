Reading the News Like a Pro

This expands on the media literacy notes already in the app's homepage — the same ideas, in more depth.

News vs. Opinion

The most common way readers get misled isn't fabricated news — it's opinion content mistaken for reporting. Reputable outlets label opinion pieces as Opinion, Editorial, Analysis, or Commentary; straight news attributes claims to named sources and keeps its adjectives boring. Opinion argues what you should think about the facts; news reports the facts. Both are legitimate genres of journalism, but conflating them is how a persuasive column gets treated as established fact.

A quick tell: if a piece uses first-person argument, predicts what will happen, or tells you what someone "should" do, it's very likely opinion regardless of what section it's filed under online.

Verify Before You Share

Read laterally. Instead of scrutinizing the article in front of you, open new tabs and see what other, unrelated outlets say about the same claim. A claim reported nowhere else is either a genuine scoop — rare — or wrong.

Find the primary source. Court rulings, bills, agency reports, and government data are public documents. When an article describes what a ruling "says," the ruling itself is usually one search away.

Check the date. Old stories recirculate on social media as if breaking, often stripped of the context that made them accurate at the time.

Reverse-image-search photos that seem too perfect — a startling image with no clear original source is a common vector for out-of-context or fabricated claims.

Understanding Bias — Including Your Own

Media bias mostly lives in story selection and framing rather than outright fabrication: which stories get covered at all, which expert gets quoted, which detail leads the headline. Two outlets can report the same underlying facts and leave completely different impressions just through emphasis and word choice.

The harder bias to manage is your own. Confirmation bias means stories that flatter your existing views get less scrutiny than stories that challenge them — apply the same skepticism in both directions. A balanced news diet across the political spectrum outperforms any single source that claims to be unbiased, because no single source actually is.

How Down the Middle Tries to Help

Every story on Down the Middle is found through live search of current reporting — nothing is pre-written from a fixed list. We describe each outlet's framing in our own words rather than reproducing their text, link to the original coverage so you can read the full piece, and generate the neutral summary independently of either side's framing rather than averaging the two. None of that makes the tool infallible — treat it as a faster way to see where coverage diverges, not a final word on what's true.
