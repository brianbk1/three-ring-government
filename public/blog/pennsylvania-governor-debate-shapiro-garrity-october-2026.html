What the Pennsylvania Governor Debate Revealed

Shapiro and Garrity offered different approaches to growth, public spending and accountability. Here are the policy choices beneath the campaign arguments.

Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro and state Treasurer Stacy Garrity met in Harrisburg on October 7 for their only scheduled gubernatorial debate. The hourlong exchange gave voters a direct comparison ahead of the November 3 election. [1]

The central disagreement was about leadership: Shapiro defended governing through a divided legislature, while Garrity argued that his unfinished priorities showed a failure to negotiate. That contrast ran through the discussion of public spending, schools and economic development. [2]

Data centers and community control

Shapiro defended his executive order establishing safeguards for data center development. Garrity favored a moratorium while communities and other stakeholders work out protections. WESA reported that she did not specify how long the pause would last. [3]

The practical distinction is between proceeding under rules already announced and pausing development to establish the next framework. Voters should ask both candidates how their approach would protect utility customers and local communities, and how those protections would be enforced.

Spending and the cost of government

Garrity criticized budgets that drew on the state surplus. Shapiro defended bipartisan budgets and challenged her to identify the programs she would cut. Spotlight PA reported that Garrity did not offer a detailed package of spending reductions or new revenue during the debate. [4]

There was also an important accounting distinction: Spotlight PA noted that the $8 billion Shapiro referenced was in the rainy day fund, rather than an ordinary surplus available for routine spending. [4]

A useful test for either platform is whether recurring commitments have recurring funding. Promises to protect services and restrain spending need a budget that shows how both goals fit together.

Education funding and school choice

Shapiro emphasized investment in public schools and said he remained open to school choice alongside those investments. Garrity criticized his changing position on vouchers and argued for a clearer commitment to choice. WITF reported that Shapiro was considering participation in a federal education tax-credit program. [5]

Those are related questions, but they should be evaluated separately: how to fund public schools, what alternatives families can access, and what accountability should accompany public support.

Gas prices and skill games

Garrity proposed a $750 summer rebate for drivers and a temporary suspension of fuel taxes. Shapiro said he was open to a gas-tax suspension but emphasized the need for legislative action. These were positions and proposals, not benefits voters could claim after the debate. [4]

On skill games, WESA reported that Shapiro supported a 52% tax, while Garrity favored a $500 monthly charge per machine. Those approaches create different obligations for operators and different revenue possibilities for the state. [3]

The political arguments and the unanswered questions

Shapiro repeatedly connected Garrity to President Donald Trump. Garrity challenged Shapiro’s record and his national ambitions. Lehigh Valley Public Media reported that Shapiro did not commit to completing a second term; it also reported that Garrity criticized aspects of Trump’s tariffs while defending his major tax-and-spending legislation. [2]

Our assessment: the most useful takeaway is a set of policy contrasts, rather than a declaration that one candidate won. A compelling answer can still lack a funding plan. A criticism can expose a weakness without explaining how the challenger would solve it.

Before choosing, ask three questions of both candidates: What would change first? What would it cost, and who would pay? What agreement would be needed to make it happen? The debate supplied some starting points. Voters should expect specific follow-through.
