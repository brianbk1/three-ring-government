Inflation Is Back at 3.7%. Is Gasoline the Whole Story?

October 16, 2026 · Independent, nonpartisan analysis

U.S. inflation accelerated in September, and gasoline was the biggest immediate reason. But the latest reports also show price pressures outside the gas station, creating a harder decision for the Federal Reserve later this month.

The question is not whether prices rose—they did. It is how much of the increase is a temporary energy shock, and how much could persist.

Gasoline explains much of the jump

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported October 14 that consumer prices rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% over the previous 12 months, up from 3.4% annual inflation in August. Gasoline prices climbed 9.1% in September and accounted for more than half the monthly increase in the overall Consumer Price Index (CPI). The broader energy index rose 5.2% in one month. [1]

Other categories moved differently. Grocery prices slipped 0.1% for the month, while restaurant prices increased 0.2%. Housing costs, represented by the shelter index, rose 0.4%. That mix is why a single national inflation number rarely matches any one household's experience. [1]

Two numbers, two different ideas: 3.7% is the increase over 12 months. The 0.6% CPI gain measures September alone. Neither means every price rose by that amount.

Underlying inflation and business costs matter, too

The 'core' CPI—which excludes food and energy—rose 0.3% in September and 2.6% over the year, compared with 2.4% the month before. It is not a perfect measure of every family's costs, but it helps policymakers see whether inflation is spreading beyond volatile fuel prices. [1]

A separate report released October 15 showed producer prices rising 1.3% for the month and 6.3% over the year. Energy was a major driver: more than 80% of the September increase in producer goods prices came from energy. Producer prices measure what domestic producers receive, not what shoppers pay. Businesses may pass higher costs along, absorb them in margins, or delay adjustments—so a producer-price spike does not automatically predict an equal jump at checkout. [2]

What it means for paychecks and interest rates

Inflation is already affecting purchasing power. BLS reported that inflation-adjusted average hourly earnings fell 0.5% from August to September and 0.7% over the year for all employees on private nonfarm payrolls. That does not mean every worker's wages declined; it means average pay, after accounting for inflation, bought slightly less. [3]

The Fed raised its policy-rate target to 3.75%–4.00% in September. There are legitimate arguments on both sides of its next move. A fuel-driven surge could fade, and raising rates further could make borrowing and hiring more difficult. On the other hand, continuing increases in core prices, housing costs and business expenses could justify keeping policy restrictive—or tightening further—to limit persistent inflation. Neither outcome is certain. [4]

What to watch next

The Federal Reserve meets October 27–28. The next CPI report, covering October, is scheduled for November 10. Watch whether gasoline stabilizes, whether core inflation eases and whether wages regain purchasing power. Those trends will be more informative than any one eye-catching monthly number. [5] [1]

For households, the practical response is to review energy and transportation spending, maintain a little budget flexibility and compare borrowing offers rather than making a major decision based on an assumed rate cut or hike.
